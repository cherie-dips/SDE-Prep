App Architecture & Data Modeling

The 45-minute round: API design, class design, data schema. 45 minutes is the common length for this round wherever it is run.

The book this guide is built on

Head First Design Patterns (Freeman & Robson, O'Reilly) is the reference used throughout. It is an excellent book for the design-patterns and system-design side of interviews — it teaches the principles behind the patterns rather than just cataloguing them. It is written entirely in Java, so every pattern here is re-expressed in modern C++, and section 5 sets out the Java-to-C++ OOP differences you need before reading the patterns: interfaces vs pure virtual base classes, garbage collection vs RAII and smart pointers, virtual destructors, value vs reference semantics, and override/final.

1. What this round actually is

45 minutes is the common length for this round at most companies that run it — long enough to cover three areas at different depths, short enough that you have to prioritise out loud. It sits between the System Design round (boxes and arrows: load balancers, caches, queues) and the Coding round (write a working function).

The distinction that trips people up. System Design asks "what machines do you need and how do they talk?" App Architecture & Data Modeling asks "inside one service, what classes, endpoints and tables do you need?"

If you start drawing load balancers in this round you are in the wrong round. If you start designing class hierarchies in the System Design round, likewise.

The three areas, and the trap in the brief

The brief for this round is usually phrased as three things you will be assessed on:

AreaWhat they said they wantWhat that means concretely
API design "The top-level interface of your application with a focus more on the input and output than the underlying details" Endpoints, request and response shapes, status codes, errors, pagination. Not implementation.
Class design "Each class serves a distinct purpose and demonstrates encapsulation and proper use of inheritance and composition" This sentence is a direct request for design patterns and OO principles. Section 6 onward.
Data schema "Tables, columns, sensible relationships between tables, and primary and foreign keys" An ER model, normalised, with the keys and indexes named explicitly.

And then the crucial line that usually accompanies it: "in the allotted 45 minutes, you will only have time to do a 'deep dive' into two out of three main areas... That being said, you should still have sufficient time to touch on each area."

Read that as an instruction, because it is one

They are telling you the winning strategy outright: touch all three, go deep on two.

The failure mode they are warning you about is spending 40 minutes lovingly designing class hierarchies and never drawing a single table. That reads as an incomplete answer even if the classes were brilliant. Budget your time deliberately and say your budget out loud at the start — it turns time management from something you might fail at into something you visibly control.

What is already in this app

Roadmap → System Design has an LLD Basics tab (OOP deep dive, composition vs inheritance, SRP & OCP, LSP/ISP/DIP) and a Patterns & Case Studies tab (Singleton, Factory, Observer, Strategy, Decorator, Adapter, Builder, Command, State, Proxy, Chain of Responsibility) plus case studies for Parking Lot, BookMyShow, Splitwise and Elevator.

Use that for the SOLID principles and for a second take on the patterns. This guide adds what that material does not have: the specific shape of this 45-minute round, C++ implementations rather than pseudocode or Java, API design, real schema design with keys and indexes, and the concurrency answers. Where a case study overlaps (Parking Lot, Elevator) treat this version as the one tuned to this round's rubric.

Which two should you go deep on?

Let the problem decide, and say why you chose:

  • Lots of entities and relationships (an e-commerce catalogue, a booking system) → go deep on data schema plus API.
  • Lots of varying behaviour (a pricing engine, a notification system, a game) → go deep on class design plus API.
  • API is almost always one of the two, because it is the contract that forces the other two to be concrete.

Announce it: "This problem is relationship-heavy, so I'd like to go deep on the schema and the API, and sketch the class design more briefly — does that work for you?" You have now demonstrated prioritisation and got their buy-in, which means they cannot mark you down for the area you skimmed.

2. The 45-minute method

Have one repeatable script so you are never improvising structure under pressure. This is the one I would use.

MinutesPhaseWhat you produce
0–6Requirements & scope A written list of functional requirements, the ones you are explicitly excluding, and the scale assumptions
6–10Entities A list of nouns — the core objects in the domain — and how they relate
10–22Deep dive 1 (usually schema) Tables with columns, types, primary and foreign keys, indexes
22–34Deep dive 2 (usually API) Endpoints with methods, paths, request/response bodies, status codes
34–40Third area, lighter Class sketch with the one or two patterns that genuinely fit
40–45Trade-offs & questions What you would change at 100× scale; what you deliberately left out; your questions

Phase 1: Requirements — the six questions

Never start designing immediately. Their rubric explicitly rewards "requirements gathering" and "handling ambiguity". Ask these:

  1. Who are the users, and what are the top three things they do? This gives you your entities and your endpoints in one answer.
  2. What is explicitly out of scope? Get permission to ignore things. "Should I handle payments, or assume a payment service exists?"
  3. Roughly what scale? Hundreds of users or hundreds of millions? This decides whether you normalise hard or denormalise for reads.
  4. Read-heavy or write-heavy? Drives indexing and caching decisions.
  5. What must be consistent, and what can be slightly stale? The single most useful question in the whole round.
  6. Is there anything unusual about this domain I should know? Free information, and it signals humility.

Then write the requirements down and get agreement

"So: users can search listings, book a slot, and cancel. Out of scope: payments, notifications, admin tools. Roughly 100k users, read-heavy, and a double-booking is unacceptable so booking must be strongly consistent. Have I got that right?"

That single paragraph hits requirements gathering, scope control, scale, and consistency — four rubric items in twenty seconds. It also protects you: anything you skip later, you skipped with their agreement.

Phase 2: Find the nouns

The reliable trick for going from a vague prompt to a concrete model: underline the nouns in the requirements. Nouns become entities, entities become tables and classes. Verbs become API endpoints and methods.

"A user can search listings and book a slot" gives you User, Listing, Slot, Booking as entities, and search / book as operations. Then ask of each pair: one-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-many? Every many-to-many becomes a join table — and spotting those early is most of data modelling.

Narrate constantly

Their rubric lists communication in the coding round, and this round is no different. Two habits that carry a session:

  • State the alternative you rejected. "I'll put the address in its own table rather than inline on the user, because a user can have several and we'll want to query by city. If addresses were single and never queried, inline would be simpler." That one sentence shows you did not just pick the first idea.
  • Flag your assumptions as assumptions. "I'm assuming a listing belongs to exactly one owner — tell me if it can be co-owned, because that changes this to a many-to-many."

3. Turning a vague prompt into a design

A worked example of the first ten minutes, because this is where most candidates lose the session before they have written anything.

Prompt: "Design a system for a library."

What a weak candidate does

Immediately writes class Book { string title; string author; } and starts adding fields. Twenty minutes later there is a class diagram, no API, no tables, and no one has established whether this library lends e-books, charges fines, or has multiple branches.

What you do instead

Step 1 — ask the six questions. Suppose the answers come back: physical books only, multiple branches, members can borrow and reserve, fines for late returns, about 50,000 members, read-heavy, and a book must never be lent to two people at once.

Step 2 — write the scope out loud.

IN SCOPE                          OUT OF SCOPE
- search the catalogue            - payments / fine collection
- borrow and return               - e-books, audiobooks
- reserve (hold) a title          - member registration flow
- fines accrue on late return     - staff / admin tooling
- multiple branches

SCALE: ~50k members, ~200k copies, read-heavy
CONSISTENCY: a copy may never be double-lent -> needs a transaction

Step 3 — the nouns, and the one distinction that matters.

Here is the modelling insight this problem is really testing, and it generalises far beyond libraries: a Book is not a Copy.

"The Hobbit" is a title — it has an author, an ISBN, a description. But you do not borrow a title, you borrow a specific physical copy sitting on a specific branch's shelf, with its own condition and barcode. One title, many copies.

Miss this and every downstream design is wrong: you cannot express "we have 3 copies, 2 are out", you cannot track which branch holds what, and you cannot lend the same title to two people correctly. Spotting the abstract-versus-physical split is the single most valuable modelling habit, and it recurs constantly: Product vs. Inventory Item, Flight vs. Seat, Movie vs. Screening, Recipe vs. Batch.

Member    Title (the work)    Copy (a physical object)    Branch
Loan      Reservation         Fine

Title  1 --- * Copy          a title has many copies
Branch 1 --- * Copy          each copy lives at one branch
Member 1 --- * Loan          a member has many loans over time
Copy   1 --- * Loan          a copy has many loans over time
   => Loan is the join between Member and Copy, plus dates

Step 4 — state your plan and time budget. "This is entity-heavy, so I'd like to spend most time on the schema and API, and sketch classes at the end. Starting with the schema."

You are now ten minutes in with a scoped problem, an agreed entity model, a stated plan, and you have already demonstrated four rubric items. Everything after this is filling in detail.

4. API design

The brief for this area: "the top-level interface of your application with a focus more on the input and output than the underlying details." So they want the contract, not the implementation. Almost always one of your two deep dives, because it forces everything else to be concrete.

The shape of a REST API

The core idea: your API exposes resources (nouns), and HTTP methods are the verbs that act on them. Get the nouns right and the endpoints write themselves.

MethodMeaningSafe?Idempotent?
GETRead; never changes anythingYesYes
POSTCreate a new item, or trigger an actionNoNo
PUTReplace an item entirelyNoYes
PATCHUpdate part of an itemNoUsually
DELETERemove an itemNoYes
Idempotent

doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once.

DELETE is idempotent: delete booking 7, then delete it again — it is still just gone. POST is not: post a booking twice and you have two bookings. This matters because networks fail. If a client sends a request and the connection drops, it does not know whether the server processed it. For an idempotent method it can just retry. For POST it cannot, without help.

The idempotency answer that impresses

When you design a "create" or "pay" endpoint, say this:

"POST isn't idempotent, so a client that times out can't safely retry — it might double-book. I'd have the client generate an idempotency key (a UUID) and send it as a header. The server stores the key with the result; if the same key arrives again it returns the original response instead of creating a second booking."

This is exactly the kind of "beyond the requirements guardrail" their System Design rubric asks for, and it applies here too. Very few candidates raise it unprompted.

Designing the endpoints

Rules that keep you out of trouble:

  • Plural nouns for collections: /books, not /getBook. The verb is the HTTP method, so putting a verb in the path is redundant.
  • Nest only to show ownership: /members/42/loans is good when loans only exist within a member. Do not nest more than two levels deep — past that, use query parameters.
  • Filtering, sorting and paging are query parameters, not paths: /books?author=tolkien&sort=-published&limit=20.
  • Actions that are not CRUD get a sub-resource: returning a book is not "update loan" in any natural sense, so POST /loans/{id}/return is clearer and honest.
GET    /books?q=hobbit&branch=3&limit=20&cursor=eyJpZCI6MTIzfQ
GET    /books/{bookId}
GET    /books/{bookId}/copies              # which copies exist, and where
POST   /loans                              # borrow: body { copyId, memberId }
POST   /loans/{loanId}/return              # an action, not a CRUD update
GET    /members/{memberId}/loans?status=active
POST   /reservations                       # body { titleId, memberId }
DELETE /reservations/{reservationId}

Two things to point out about that error shape: a machine-readable code so clients can branch on it without string-matching the message, and structured details so the client can show something useful. Say "I'd use a consistent error envelope across every endpoint" — consistency is itself a design decision.

Pagination — offset vs cursor

Offset pagination

?limit=20&offset=40 — "skip 40, give me 20".

Simple, allows jumping to page 5. Two real problems: the database must count through all skipped rows, so deep pages get slow; and if an item is inserted while the user pages, rows shift and they see a duplicate or miss one.

Cursor (keyset) pagination

?limit=20&cursor=abc — "give me 20 after this exact item".

The cursor encodes the sort key of the last row seen, so the query becomes WHERE (created_at, id) < (...) ORDER BY ... LIMIT 20, which uses the index and stays fast at any depth. Cannot jump to an arbitrary page. This is what large APIs use.

Saying "offset for an admin table where they want page numbers, cursor for an infinite feed" shows you pick by use case rather than by habit.

Versioning

Have an opinion, briefly: /v1/books in the path is the most common and the easiest to reason about; a header (Accept: application/vnd.api+json;version=1) is purer REST but harder to test by hand. The important part is the principle: additive changes don't need a new version; removing or renaming a field does.

The rest of the checklist

  • Authentication — a bearer token (JWT) in the Authorization header; mention that the API validates it and derives the caller's identity rather than trusting a memberId in the body. That last point is a genuine security instinct: never let the client tell you who it is.
  • Rate limiting — per API key, returning 429 with a Retry-After.
  • Long operations — return 202 with a job id and let the client poll /jobs/{id}, rather than holding a request open.
  • Bulk endpoints — if a client would loop over 1,000 calls, give them one POST /loans/bulk. Spotting the N+1 call pattern is a nice touch.

5. Class design in C++

About the book, before you start

Head First Design Patterns (Freeman & Robson, O'Reilly) is the source for the patterns in this guide, and it is a genuinely good book for system design and low-level design — it teaches you why each pattern exists and what breaks without it, instead of handing you a catalogue of UML diagrams to memorise. Worth reading end to end.

The one catch: every example in it is written in Java. Java and C++ differ in exactly the places OOP interviews probe, so a pattern transliterated straight from the book will read as Java-with-different-syntax. This section is the translation layer — the OOP concepts compared between Java and C++ — and every pattern in the following sections is written in modern C++ rather than converted Java. Read this section before the patterns.

The rest of this section works through the ones that change how you actually write the pattern.

1. C++ has no interface keyword

Java's interface Duck { void quack(); } becomes an abstract base class with pure virtual functions:

class QuackBehavior {
public:
    virtual ~QuackBehavior() = default;   // <-- REQUIRED. see below
    virtual void quack() const = 0;       // "= 0" makes it pure virtual
};

class LoudQuack : public QuackBehavior {
public:
    void quack() const override { std::cout << "QUACK!\n"; }
};

The virtual destructor rule — they will check this

If you delete a derived object through a base pointer and the base destructor is not virtual, the behaviour is undefined — in practice the derived part never gets destroyed and you leak.

Base* p = new Derived();
delete p;          // Base::~Base not virtual -> Derived::~Derived never runs

Rule: any class you intend to inherit from polymorphically gets a virtual ~Class() = default;. Write it every time, unprompted. In an interview it takes two seconds and it is the single most reliable signal that you actually write C++.

2. Ownership replaces garbage collection

Java hands you a reference and the collector cleans up. In C++ you must say who owns what, and that choice is part of the design.

TypeMeaningUse for
std::unique_ptr<T>Exactly one owner; freed when it goes away The default. A Decorator wrapping its component, a Strategy held by a Context
std::shared_ptr<T>Shared ownership, reference counted When lifetime genuinely is shared — several observers holding a subject
std::weak_ptr<T>Observes without owning; can detect death Observer lists, and breaking reference cycles
T& or T*Non-owning reference A parameter you only read; a back-pointer to a parent

The interview line: "I'll hold this by unique_ptr because ownership is exclusive; if I needed shared ownership I'd use shared_ptr, but I'd reach for that deliberately rather than by default." Reaching for shared_ptr everywhere is the classic sign of someone writing Java in C++.

3. Rule of Zero, Three, Five

Rule of Zero

if your class holds its resources in types that already manage themselves (std::string, std::vector, smart pointers), write no destructor, copy constructor or assignment operator. The compiler's defaults are correct.

This is what you want almost always. Say "Rule of Zero" and move on.

Rule of Five

if you do write one of {destructor, copy constructor, copy assignment, move constructor, move assignment}, you probably need to consider all five.

Comes up when you hand-manage a resource. In a design round you should usually be avoiding that.

4. Copies are real, and they cost

Java passes object references. C++ copies by default. So void f(std::string s) copies the whole string; void f(const std::string& s) does not. Get this wrong in an interview and it looks like you learned C++ from Java. Defaults to use:

  • Read-only parameter → const T&
  • The function will keep a copy → take by value and std::move it in
  • Cheap types (int, double, an enum) → by value

5. std::function often replaces a whole class hierarchy

A genuinely idiomatic C++ move that the book cannot show you. If a Strategy has one method and no state, you do not need an inheritance hierarchy at all:

// The book's way: an interface plus a class per behaviour.
// The C++ way, when the behaviour is a single function:
class Duck {
    std::function<void()> quack_;
public:
    explicit Duck(std::function<void()> q) : quack_(std::move(q)) {}
    void performQuack() const { quack_(); }
};

Duck loud([]{ std::cout << "QUACK!\n"; });
Duck mute([]{ /* nothing */ });

When to prefer the class hierarchy anyway: the behaviour has state, several related methods, or you want to name and test the behaviours independently. Being able to argue both sides is the point — their rubric explicitly rewards discussing technical trade-offs.

6. Small keywords that signal care

  • override — on every function overriding a virtual. The compiler then catches a typo'd signature that would otherwise silently create a new function.
  • final — on classes not designed for further inheritance.
  • explicit — on single-argument constructors, to stop surprise implicit conversions.
  • enum class Status { Active, Returned }; — scoped and type-safe, unlike a plain enum.
  • const on member functions that do not mutate. Const-correctness is free credibility.
  • [[nodiscard]] on functions whose return value must not be ignored.

7. Composition in C++

// Inheritance: Manager IS-A Employee
class Manager : public Employee { ... };

// Composition: Order HAS-A payment method
class Order {
    std::unique_ptr<PaymentMethod> payment_;   // swappable at runtime
    std::vector<LineItem>          items_;     // owns its items by value
};

The test to say out loud: "is-a" means inheritance, "has-a" means composition — and if you are ever unsure, prefer composition, because it can be changed at runtime and does not couple you to a base class's internals.

6. The nine design principles

These are the principles Head First Design Patterns builds everything on, quoted as the book states them. The patterns are just recurring applications of these. If you remember nothing else, remember the first three — they generate most good designs on their own.

1. Identify the aspects of your application that vary and separate them from what stays the same.

find the part that keeps changing, and pull it out into its own thing.

The book's master principle; every pattern is an instance of it. In the interview, the question to ask yourself about any design is "what is most likely to change here?" Then make that the part behind an interface. New payment method, new discount rule, new export format — these are the axes of change, and they should each be one new class rather than an edit to five.

2. Program to an interface, not an implementation.

depend on what something does, not on which concrete class does it.

// Bad: bound to one concrete class forever
MySQLBookRepo repo_;

// Good: any implementation will do - including a fake one in tests
std::unique_ptr<BookRepository> repo_;

The immediate practical payoff to mention: testability. If your service depends on an interface you can inject an in-memory fake and test it without a database. Interviewers love a candidate who links a design principle to testing.

3. Favor composition over inheritance.

build behaviour by holding other objects, rather than by inheriting from a base class.

Why the book pushes this so hard: inheritance is fixed at compile time and applies to all instances of the class, while composition can be swapped at runtime. Inheritance also creates the tightest possible coupling — a change to the base class ripples into every subclass. And deep hierarchies produce the class explosion the book opens with (a subclass for every combination of features).

4. Strive for loosely coupled designs between objects that interact.

objects should know as little about each other as possible.

Observer is the poster child: the subject knows only that observers implement an interface. It does not know their concrete types, how many there are, or what they do. You can add new observers without touching the subject at all.

5. Classes should be open for extension, but closed for modification.

you should be able to add new behaviour by adding new code, not by editing existing, working, tested code.

Known as the Open-Closed Principle. The tell that you are violating it is a growing if/else or switch on a type tag — every new case means editing that function again. Replacing that switch with polymorphism is the single most common refactor in this interview round.

6. Depend upon abstractions. Do not depend upon concrete classes.

both high-level and low-level code should depend on interfaces in the middle.

The Dependency Inversion Principle. "Inversion" because naively your high-level OrderService would depend on the low-level MySQLRepo; instead you define a Repository interface owned by the high-level code, and the database class implements it. The arrow of dependency now points up, and you can swap the database without touching the business logic.

7. Principle of Least Knowledge — talk only to your immediate friends.

don't reach through one object to get at another.

// Violates it - now you depend on Order, Customer AND Address
order.getCustomer().getAddress().getCity()

// Better: ask the object you know, for what you need
order.shippingCity()

Also called the Law of Demeter. The chain is fragile: any of those three classes changing breaks your code. Spotting a "train wreck" call chain in a design discussion is a nice catch.

8. Don't call us, we'll call you (the Hollywood Principle).

the framework calls your code; your code doesn't call the framework.

This is what Template Method does: the base class owns the algorithm's skeleton and calls down into your overridden steps at the right moments. You never call the skeleton yourself.

9. A class should have only one reason to change.

each class does one job.

The Single Responsibility Principle. The practical test: describe the class in one sentence. If you need the word "and", it probably wants splitting. A class that formats a report and emails it has two reasons to change — a formatting change and a delivery change — and they will fight.

How to actually use these in the room

Do not recite them. Use them as justifications. When you make a design decision, name the principle behind it in half a sentence:

"I'll put the discount rules behind an interface — that's the part most likely to change, and it keeps the order code closed for modification when marketing invents a new promotion."

You have just cited principles 1 and 5 without sounding like a textbook.

7. The patterns you will actually use

Seven patterns cover the overwhelming majority of what this round asks for. Each one below has: the problem it solves, the book's definition, working C++, and — just as important — when not to reach for it.

The warning that matters most. The commonest way to fail a design round is to bolt on patterns to show you know them. A Singleton nobody needed, a Factory producing one product, an Observer with one observer. Interviewers read that as cargo-culting. Name a pattern only when you can say what would go wrong without it.

Strategy

The problem: you have a family of interchangeable algorithms — several ways to calculate shipping, several ways to sort, several discount rules — and you keep editing a growing if/else every time a new one appears.

The book: "defines a family of algorithms, encapsulates each one, and makes them interchangeable. Strategy lets the algorithm vary independently from clients that use it."

#include <memory>
#include <utility>

class ShippingStrategy {
public:
    virtual ~ShippingStrategy() = default;
    virtual double cost(double weightKg, double distanceKm) const = 0;
};

class StandardShipping : public ShippingStrategy {
public:
    double cost(double w, double d) const override { return 3.0 + 0.5 * w + 0.01 * d; }
};

class ExpressShipping : public ShippingStrategy {
public:
    double cost(double w, double d) const override { return 9.0 + 1.2 * w + 0.03 * d; }
};

class Order {
    std::unique_ptr<ShippingStrategy> shipping_;
public:
    explicit Order(std::unique_ptr<ShippingStrategy> s) : shipping_(std::move(s)) {}

    // swappable at RUNTIME - the thing inheritance cannot do
    void setShipping(std::unique_ptr<ShippingStrategy> s) { shipping_ = std::move(s); }

    double shippingCost(double w, double d) const { return shipping_->cost(w, d); }
};

Where it shows up in this round: pricing, discounts, ranking, routing, retry policies, export formats. Any time the interviewer says "and we might want to add other kinds of X later", they are asking for Strategy.

Don't use it when there is exactly one algorithm and no realistic prospect of a second. And remember section 5: if the strategy is one stateless function, std::function is the lighter C++ answer.

Observer

The problem: when something happens, several unrelated parts of the system need to react — and you do not want the thing that changed to know about all of them.

The book: "defines a one-to-many dependency between objects so that when one object changes state, all of its dependents are notified and updated automatically."

#include <memory>
#include <vector>
#include <string>
#include <algorithm>

class OrderObserver {
public:
    virtual ~OrderObserver() = default;
    virtual void onOrderPlaced(const std::string& orderId) = 0;
};

class OrderService {
    // weak_ptr: we watch observers, we do NOT own them, and we can tell
    // when one has been destroyed. This is the C++-specific bit.
    std::vector<std::weak_ptr<OrderObserver>> observers_;
public:
    void subscribe(std::shared_ptr<OrderObserver> o) {
        observers_.push_back(std::move(o));
    }

    void placeOrder(const std::string& orderId) {
        // ... persist the order ...
        notify(orderId);
    }

private:
    void notify(const std::string& orderId) {
        // prune dead observers while notifying the live ones
        auto dead = std::remove_if(observers_.begin(), observers_.end(),
            [&](const std::weak_ptr<OrderObserver>& w) {
                if (auto o = w.lock()) { o->onOrderPlaced(orderId); return false; }
                return true;                       // expired -> remove
            });
        observers_.erase(dead, observers_.end());
    }
};

class EmailNotifier : public OrderObserver {
public:
    void onOrderPlaced(const std::string& id) override { /* send email */ }
};
class InventoryUpdater : public OrderObserver {
public:
    void onOrderPlaced(const std::string& id) override { /* decrement stock */ }
};

Say the weak_ptr reasoning out loud. "I hold observers by weak_ptr so the subject doesn't keep them alive — otherwise you get a leak, or a cycle if an observer also holds the subject. Locking the weak pointer also gives me a natural place to prune dead ones." In Java the collector handles this; in C++ it is a design decision, and noticing it is a strong signal.

Where it shows up: "when an order is placed we also need to email the customer, update inventory, and notify the warehouse." Three things reacting to one event is the tell.

Don't use it when there is one listener and there always will be — a direct call is clearer. Also be honest about the downside: notification order is not guaranteed and debugging becomes harder, because control flow is no longer local.

Decorator

The problem: you need to add optional responsibilities in arbitrary combinations. With inheritance you would need a subclass per combination — the class explosion the book opens with.

The book: "attaches additional responsibilities to an object dynamically. Decorators provide a flexible alternative to subclassing for extending functionality."

#include <memory>
#include <string>
#include <utility>

class Beverage {
public:
    virtual ~Beverage() = default;
    virtual std::string description() const = 0;
    virtual double cost() const = 0;
};

class Espresso : public Beverage {
public:
    std::string description() const override { return "Espresso"; }
    double cost() const override { return 1.99; }
};

// The decorator IS-A Beverage and HAS-A Beverage. That dual nature is the whole trick:
// being a Beverage lets it stand in anywhere; having one lets it wrap.
class CondimentDecorator : public Beverage {
protected:
    std::unique_ptr<Beverage> inner_;
public:
    explicit CondimentDecorator(std::unique_ptr<Beverage> b) : inner_(std::move(b)) {}
};

class Mocha : public CondimentDecorator {
public:
    using CondimentDecorator::CondimentDecorator;
    std::string description() const override { return inner_->description() + ", Mocha"; }
    double cost() const override { return inner_->cost() + 0.20; }
};

class Whip : public CondimentDecorator {
public:
    using CondimentDecorator::CondimentDecorator;
    std::string description() const override { return inner_->description() + ", Whip"; }
    double cost() const override { return inner_->cost() + 0.10; }
};

// Espresso, Mocha, Mocha, Whip - stacked at runtime, no combinatorial subclasses
inline std::unique_ptr<Beverage> makeDrink() {
    std::unique_ptr<Beverage> d = std::make_unique<Espresso>();
    d = std::make_unique<Mocha>(std::move(d));
    d = std::make_unique<Mocha>(std::move(d));
    d = std::make_unique<Whip>(std::move(d));
    return d;
}

Where it shows up: pricing with add-ons, middleware chains (logging wrapping auth wrapping the handler), compression or encryption wrapping a stream, feature toppings.

Don't use it when the combinations are few and fixed — three flags on a struct beat a wrapper tower. The honest downside: a stack of decorators is hard to debug and you lose the concrete type of what is inside.

Factory Method

The problem: your code needs to create objects, but which concrete class depends on context — and you do not want new ConcreteThing scattered everywhere, because that is depending on a concrete class (principle 6).

The book: "defines an interface for creating an object, but lets subclasses decide which class to instantiate."

#include <memory>
#include <string>

class Pizza {
public:
    virtual ~Pizza() = default;
    virtual void prepare() = 0;
};
class NYCheesePizza : public Pizza { public: void prepare() override {} };
class ChicagoCheesePizza : public Pizza { public: void prepare() override {} };

class PizzaStore {
public:
    virtual ~PizzaStore() = default;

    // the algorithm lives here, once
    std::unique_ptr<Pizza> orderPizza(const std::string& type) {
        auto pizza = createPizza(type);   // ...but the CREATION is deferred
        pizza->prepare();
        return pizza;
    }
protected:
    // the factory method: subclasses decide what gets built
    virtual std::unique_ptr<Pizza> createPizza(const std::string& type) = 0;
};

class NYPizzaStore : public PizzaStore {
protected:
    std::unique_ptr<Pizza> createPizza(const std::string&) override {
        return std::make_unique<NYCheesePizza>();
    }
};

Simpler cousin worth naming: a static factory function — one function with a switch that returns the right unique_ptr. Not a GoF pattern, but often the right answer, and saying "a simple factory function is enough here" shows judgement rather than pattern-hunger.

Abstract Factory

The problem: you need to create families of related objects that must be used together, and mixing families would be a bug.

The book: "provides an interface for creating families of related or dependent objects without specifying their concrete classes."

// the product interfaces, and one family's concrete versions
class Dough  { public: virtual ~Dough()  = default; };
class Sauce  { public: virtual ~Sauce()  = default; };
class Cheese { public: virtual ~Cheese() = default; };
class ThinCrust : public Dough  {};
class Marinara  : public Sauce  {};
class Reggiano  : public Cheese {};

class IngredientFactory {
public:
    virtual ~IngredientFactory() = default;
    virtual std::unique_ptr<Dough>  createDough()  = 0;
    virtual std::unique_ptr<Sauce>  createSauce()  = 0;
    virtual std::unique_ptr<Cheese> createCheese() = 0;
};

class NYIngredientFactory : public IngredientFactory {
public:
    std::unique_ptr<Dough>  createDough()  override { return std::make_unique<ThinCrust>(); }
    std::unique_ptr<Sauce>  createSauce()  override { return std::make_unique<Marinara>(); }
    std::unique_ptr<Cheese> createCheese() override { return std::make_unique<Reggiano>(); }
};

Factory Method vs Abstract Factory — they will ask. Factory Method creates one product and varies it through inheritance (a subclass overrides the method). Abstract Factory creates a family of products and varies it through composition (you hand the object a different factory). One product, one method; a family, an object.

Command

The problem: you want to treat "do this thing" as an object — so you can queue it, log it, schedule it, or undo it.

The book: "encapsulates a request as an object, thereby letting you parameterize other objects with different requests, queue or log requests, and support undoable operations."

#include <memory>
#include <vector>

class Command {
public:
    virtual ~Command() = default;
    virtual void execute() = 0;
    virtual void undo()    = 0;      // undo is what makes Command earn its keep
};

class Light {
public:
    void on()  {}
    void off() {}
};

class LightOnCommand : public Command {
    Light& light_;                    // non-owning: the receiver outlives the command
public:
    explicit LightOnCommand(Light& l) : light_(l) {}
    void execute() override { light_.on();  }
    void undo()    override { light_.off(); }
};

class RemoteControl {
    std::vector<std::unique_ptr<Command>> history_;
public:
    void submit(std::unique_ptr<Command> c) {
        c->execute();
        history_.push_back(std::move(c));
    }
    void undoLast() {
        if (history_.empty()) return;
        history_.back()->undo();
        history_.pop_back();
    }
};

Where it shows up: undo/redo, job queues, transactional operations, request logging for replay after a crash, macro recording. If the interviewer mentions undo or a queue of work, this is the pattern.

Singleton

The problem: exactly one instance must exist — one configuration object, one connection pool.

The book: "ensures a class has only one instance, and provides a global point of access to it."

class Config {
public:
    // "Meyers singleton": thread-safe initialisation is guaranteed by the
    // standard since C++11, so no locking is needed. Prefer this always.
    static Config& instance() {
        static Config c;      // constructed once, on first use
        return c;
    }
    Config(const Config&)            = delete;   // no copying
    Config& operator=(const Config&) = delete;
private:
    Config() = default;
};

Say the criticism before they do. "It's a Meyers singleton, which is thread-safe since C++11. That said, singletons are effectively global state — they hide dependencies and make testing hard, because you can't substitute a fake. In a real design I'd usually prefer to create one instance at startup and inject it as a dependency."

Volunteering the downside of the pattern you just used is one of the strongest moves available in a design interview.

8. The rest of the pattern catalogue

Less frequent in this round, but each has a scenario where it is clearly the right answer. Know the one-line definition and the trigger for each.

Adapter

The book: "converts the interface of a class into another interface the clients expect. Adapter lets classes work together that couldn't otherwise because of incompatible interfaces."

Trigger: a third-party library or legacy class whose interface does not match yours, and you cannot change it.

// Their interface (can't change it)
class LegacyPaymentGateway {
public:
    bool doPayment(const char* cardNo, int cents) { return true; }
};

// Our interface
class PaymentProcessor {
public:
    virtual ~PaymentProcessor() = default;
    virtual bool charge(const std::string& card, double amount) = 0;
};

class LegacyGatewayAdapter : public PaymentProcessor {
    LegacyPaymentGateway& gateway_;
public:
    explicit LegacyGatewayAdapter(LegacyPaymentGateway& g) : gateway_(g) {}
    bool charge(const std::string& card, double amount) override {
        return gateway_.doPayment(card.c_str(), static_cast<int>(amount * 100));
    }
};

Facade

The book: "provides a unified interface to a set of interfaces in a subsystem. Facade defines a higher-level interface that makes the subsystem easier to use."

Trigger: a client has to orchestrate six objects in the right order to do one common task. Adapter vs Facade: Adapter changes an interface to match what a client expects; Facade simplifies a complicated one. Different intent, similar mechanics.

// collaborators, elided. PaymentProcessor is the interface from the Adapter example
// above - so the adapted legacy gateway drops straight in here.
class Cart { public: double total() const { return 0.0; } };
class InventoryService { public: bool reserve(const Cart&){ return true; } void release(const Cart&){} };
class ShippingService  { public: void schedule(const Cart&){} };

class OrderFacade {
    InventoryService& inventory_;
    PaymentProcessor& payment_;      // an interface, so any implementation works
    ShippingService&  shipping_;
public:
    OrderFacade(InventoryService& i, PaymentProcessor& p, ShippingService& s)
        : inventory_(i), payment_(p), shipping_(s) {}

    // one call instead of the client knowing the whole dance
    bool placeOrder(const Cart& cart, const std::string& card) {
        if (!inventory_.reserve(cart)) return false;
        if (!payment_.charge(card, cart.total())) {
            inventory_.release(cart);            // undo the reservation on failure
            return false;
        }
        shipping_.schedule(cart);
        return true;
    }
};

Template Method

The book: "defines the skeleton of an algorithm in a method, deferring some steps to subclasses. Template Method lets subclasses redefine certain steps of an algorithm without changing the algorithm's structure."

Trigger: several processes that follow the same sequence but differ in a couple of steps — importing CSV vs XML, or any "fetch, validate, transform, save" pipeline.

struct Row {};                    // a parsed record

class DataImporter {
public:
    virtual ~DataImporter() = default;

    // non-virtual: the skeleton is fixed. Subclasses cannot reorder it.
    void import(const std::string& path) {
        auto raw  = read(path);        // varies
        auto rows = parse(raw);        // varies
        validate(rows);                // shared
        save(rows);                    // shared
        if (shouldArchive()) archive(path);   // a "hook" with a default
    }
protected:
    virtual std::string read(const std::string& path) = 0;
    virtual std::vector<Row> parse(const std::string& raw) = 0;
    virtual bool shouldArchive() const { return false; }   // hook: optional override
private:
    void validate(const std::vector<Row>&) {}
    void save(const std::vector<Row>&) {}
    void archive(const std::string&) {}
};

Note the hook — a virtual with a default implementation that subclasses may override but need not. And note this is the Hollywood Principle in action: the base class calls down into you.

Template Method vs Strategy: Template Method varies steps within a fixed algorithm using inheritance. Strategy swaps the whole algorithm using composition.

Iterator

The book: "provides a way to access the elements of an aggregate object sequentially without exposing its underlying representation."

In C++ this is mostly already solved: the STL is built on iterators, so the idiomatic answer is to expose begin() and end() and let range-for work.

struct Song {};

class Playlist {
    std::vector<Song> songs_;
public:
    // Now: for (const auto& s : playlist) { ... }  - and every STL algorithm works.
    auto begin()       { return songs_.begin(); }
    auto end()         { return songs_.end();   }
    auto begin() const { return songs_.begin(); }
    auto end()   const { return songs_.end();   }
};

The point to make: the caller iterates without knowing whether you store a vector, a list or a database cursor. You can change the container without breaking anyone.

Composite

The book: "allows you to compose objects into tree structures to represent part-whole hierarchies. Composite lets clients treat individual objects and compositions of objects uniformly."

Trigger: anything tree-shaped — a filesystem, a menu with submenus, an org chart, nested UI components, a bill of materials.

class FileNode {
public:
    virtual ~FileNode() = default;
    virtual std::string name() const = 0;
    virtual long        size() const = 0;     // leaf and composite answer identically
};

class File : public FileNode {
    std::string name_; long size_;
public:
    File(std::string n, long s) : name_(std::move(n)), size_(s) {}
    std::string name() const override { return name_; }
    long        size() const override { return size_; }
};

class Directory : public FileNode {
    std::string name_;
    std::vector<std::unique_ptr<FileNode>> children_;
public:
    explicit Directory(std::string n) : name_(std::move(n)) {}
    void add(std::unique_ptr<FileNode> c) { children_.push_back(std::move(c)); }
    std::string name() const override { return name_; }
    long size() const override {                 // recursion, transparent to the caller
        long total = 0;
        for (const auto& c : children_) total += c->size();
        return total;
    }
};

State

The book: "allows an object to alter its behavior when its internal state changes. The object will appear to change its class."

Trigger: a big switch (status) repeated in several methods. An order that is Pending, Paid, Shipped, Delivered, Cancelled, where what each operation does depends on which state it is in.

class OrderContext;               // forward declaration is enough for a reference

class OrderState {
public:
    virtual ~OrderState() = default;
    virtual void pay(OrderContext&)    {}   // by default, invalid transitions do nothing
    virtual void cancel(OrderContext&) {}
    virtual const char* name() const = 0;
};

class PendingState : public OrderState {
public:
    void pay(OrderContext& ctx) override;      // -> PaidState
    void cancel(OrderContext& ctx) override;   // -> CancelledState
    const char* name() const override { return "PENDING"; }
};

class ShippedState : public OrderState {
public:
    // pay() and cancel() intentionally not overridden: you cannot cancel a shipped order.
    const char* name() const override { return "SHIPPED"; }
};

State vs Strategy — the book calls them "twins separated at birth", and it is a favourite interview question. Structurally identical; the difference is intent and who decides. With Strategy, the client chooses the algorithm and it normally stays put. With State, the objects themselves drive the transitions, so the behaviour changes over the object's lifetime without the client doing anything.

Proxy

The book: "provides a surrogate or placeholder for another object to control access to it."

Trigger: you want to add access control, caching, lazy loading or remote calls without changing the real object or its callers. Flavours: virtual proxy (create the expensive thing on first use), protection proxy (check permissions), remote proxy (a local stand-in for something on another machine), caching proxy.

class Image {
public:
    virtual ~Image() = default;
    virtual void display() const = 0;
};

class RealImage : public Image {          // expensive: loads from disk on construction
public:
    explicit RealImage(std::string path) {}
    void display() const override {}
};

class ImageProxy : public Image {
    std::string path_;
    mutable std::unique_ptr<RealImage> real_;   // mutable: created inside a const method
public:
    explicit ImageProxy(std::string p) : path_(std::move(p)) {}
    void display() const override {
        if (!real_) real_ = std::make_unique<RealImage>(path_);  // load on first use only
        real_->display();
    }
};

Proxy vs Decorator — also asked. Both wrap an object with the same interface. Decorator adds behaviour; Proxy controls access and normally does not change what the operation means.

Compound: MVC

The book's finale: Model-View-Controller is not one pattern but several working together.

  • Model notifies views of changes — that is Observer.
  • Controller is a Strategy for the view: swap the controller and the same view behaves differently.
  • Nested view components form a Composite.

Worth a sentence if you design anything with a UI, because it demonstrates that patterns combine — which is the book's real thesis.

9. Data modeling and schema design

The brief for this area: "tables, columns, sensible relationships between tables, and primary and foreign keys." That is a precise list, so hit every item explicitly — name your primary keys, name your foreign keys, and say what each relationship is.

Step 1: entities and relationships

Cardinality

how many of one thing relate to how many of another. The three cases are one-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-many.

RelationshipHow to implement itExample
One-to-many (1:N) Foreign key on the many side, pointing at the one A branch has many copies → copy.branch_id
Many-to-many (M:N) A separate join table holding both foreign keys Books and authors → book_author(book_id, author_id)
One-to-one (1:1) Usually the same table. Split only to isolate rarely-used or sensitive columns User and user_credentials

The move that earns marks: when you spot a many-to-many, say "that's many-to-many, so it needs a join table — and the join table is usually a real entity with its own data."

A student enrolling on a course is not just (student_id, course_id); the enrolment has a date, a grade, a status. Recognising that the join table wants columns of its own is the difference between a schema that works and one that has to be rebuilt.

Step 2: keys

Primary key

the column (or columns) that uniquely identify a row.

Surrogate keys are meaningless ids you generate (an auto-increment integer or a UUID). Natural keys are real-world values (an ISBN, an email). Prefer surrogate: natural keys have an unfortunate habit of changing, and then every foreign key referencing them has to change too. Keep the natural key as a UNIQUE column instead.

Foreign key

a column holding another table's primary key, with the database enforcing that the referenced row actually exists.

Also decide what happens on delete: ON DELETE CASCADE (delete the children too), RESTRICT (refuse while children exist), or SET NULL. Saying which one you want, and why, is a detail most candidates skip.

Auto-increment integer vs UUID: integers are compact and index well but leak volume information and are awkward across distributed systems; UUIDs are globally unique and can be generated client-side (which pairs nicely with idempotency keys) but are larger and randomly ordered, which hurts index locality. Have the trade-off ready.

Step 3: normalisation

Normalisation

organising columns so each fact is stored in exactly one place, so it cannot disagree with itself.

  • 1NF — no repeating groups; each cell holds a single value. No phone1, phone2, phone3 columns and no comma-separated lists.
  • 2NF — 1NF, and every non-key column depends on the whole primary key. Only bites when the key is composite.
  • 3NF — 2NF, and no non-key column depends on another non-key column. If you store city and postcode and the postcode determines the city, that is a violation.

The one-liner to remember: "every non-key column depends on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key." 3NF is the target for a normal transactional schema.

Then say when you would break it

Normalisation prevents update anomalies but costs joins. Denormalise deliberately when reads dominate and the data is stable:

  • Counters — storing comment_count on a post beats counting a million rows, at the cost of keeping it in step.
  • Point-in-time snapshots — and this one is not really denormalisation at all. An order line must store the price at the time of purchase, not join to the product's current price. If the product's price changes next week, every historical invoice must not change with it. This is the single most commonly missed modelling point in e-commerce questions, and raising it unprompted is a strong signal.

Step 4: column types and the details that catch people

DataUseNot
MoneyDECIMAL(12,2), or an integer count of minor units FLOAT — binary floating point cannot represent 0.10 exactly, so sums drift
TimestampsTIMESTAMPTZ, always stored UTC Local time with no zone — unrecoverable ambiguity

IdentifiersBIGINT or UUID INT, if there is any chance of exceeding 2.1 billion

The money as float point is worth stating out loud in any commerce question. It is a real bug, it is common, and knowing it reads as production experience.

Step 5: indexes

Index

a sorted side-structure that lets the database find matching rows without scanning the whole table — like a book's index.

The trade: reads get faster, writes get slower (every insert must update every index), and disk usage grows. So index deliberately, not everywhere.

What to index: every foreign key (you will join on it); columns in WHERE clauses you run often; columns you sort by.

Composite index and column order

an index on several columns at once. The order matters enormously.

An index on (member_id, status) helps a query filtering on member_id alone, and one filtering on both — but not one filtering on status alone. It is like a phone book sorted by surname then first name: useless for finding everyone called "James" if you do not know the surname. Say "leftmost prefix rule" and you will have said something most candidates cannot.

Step 6: constraints and concurrency

Push correctness into the database where you can, because application code has bugs and races:

  • NOT NULL on everything that genuinely must be present.
  • UNIQUE on natural keys — and this is how you prevent double-booking. A unique constraint on (copy_id) WHERE status = 'ACTIVE' (a partial unique index) makes it physically impossible for one copy to have two active loans, no matter what the application does.
  • CHECK for invariants, e.g. CHECK (due_at > borrowed_at).

The double-booking answer

Almost every problem in this round has a "two people grab the last one" moment. Have this ready:

"The read-then-write is a race: two requests both see the copy as available and both insert a loan. Three defences, and I'd use more than one. First, a transaction with the check and the insert inside it. Second, either pessimistic locking (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the copy row, blocking the second request) or optimistic locking (a version column; the second write fails and retries). Third, and most robust, a unique constraint so that even if the logic is wrong the database refuses the second row — and I'd return 409 Conflict when it does."

Pessimistic suits high contention and short transactions; optimistic suits low contention. Naming both and choosing is what they want.

Step 7: the questions that show seniority

  • Soft deletes — a deleted_at column instead of really deleting, so you keep history and can undo. Cost: every query must remember to filter it.
  • Audit trail — created_at, updated_at, created_by on anything a human edits.
  • History vs current state — does the business need to know what a row used to say? If so, an events or history table, not just an updated row.
  • Multi-tenancy — if several customers share the database, a tenant_id on every table and in every index.

Indexes, and why each one exists

CREATE INDEX idx_copy_title_branch ON copy(title_id, branch_id);
    -- "which copies of this title are at this branch?" - the main catalogue query

CREATE INDEX idx_loan_member_active ON loan(member_id) WHERE returned_at IS NULL;
    -- "what does this member currently have out?" - a partial index, so it stays small

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX uniq_active_loan_per_copy ON loan(copy_id) WHERE returned_at IS NULL;
    -- THE IMPORTANT ONE. A copy can have at most one open loan. The database now makes
    -- double-lending physically impossible, whatever the application code does.

Point at that last index and say: "This is how I'd stop double-lending. Two concurrent borrow requests can both pass an availability check, but only one can insert the row — the second violates the unique index, and I turn that into a 409 Conflict."

#include <memory>

#include <string>

enum class VehicleType { Motorcycle, Car, Bus, Electric };
enum class SpotType    { Motorcycle, Compact, Large, Electric };

class Vehicle {
    std::string plate_;
    VehicleType type_;
public:
    Vehicle(std::string plate, VehicleType t) : plate_(std::move(plate)), type_(t) {}
    const std::string& plate() const { return plate_; }
    VehicleType type()         const { return type_; }
};

class ParkingSpot {
    long     id_;
    SpotType type_;
    bool     occupied_ = false;
public:
    ParkingSpot(long id, SpotType t) : id_(id), type_(t) {}
    long     id()   const { return id_; }
    SpotType type() const { return type_; }
    bool     free() const { return !occupied_; }
    void occupy()  { occupied_ = true;  }
    void release() { occupied_ = false; }

    // Which vehicles fit here. Kept as data, not as an if-chain scattered around.
    bool fits(VehicleType v) const {
        switch (type_) {
            case SpotType::Motorcycle: return v == VehicleType::Motorcycle;
            case SpotType::Compact:    return v == VehicleType::Motorcycle || v == VehicleType::Car;
            case SpotType::Large:      return true;
            case SpotType::Electric:   return v == VehicleType::Electric;
        }
        return false;
    }
};

Strategy #1: how we choose a spot

class SpotAllocationStrategy {
public:
    virtual ~SpotAllocationStrategy() = default;
    virtual std::optional<long> choose(const std::vector<ParkingSpot>& spots,
                                       VehicleType v) const = 0;
};

class FirstFitStrategy : public SpotAllocationStrategy {
public:
    std::optional<long> choose(const std::vector<ParkingSpot>& spots,
                               VehicleType v) const override {
        for (const auto& s : spots)
            if (s.free() && s.fits(v)) return s.id();
        return std::nullopt;
    }
};

// Prefer the tightest fit so big spots stay free for big vehicles.
class BestFitStrategy : public SpotAllocationStrategy {
public:
    std::optional<long> choose(const std::vector<ParkingSpot>& spots,
                               VehicleType v) const override {
        const ParkingSpot* best = nullptr;
        for (const auto& s : spots) {
            if (!s.free() || !s.fits(v)) continue;
            if (!best || static_cast<int>(s.type()) < static_cast<int>(best->type()))
                best = &s;
        }
        return best ? std::optional<long>(best->id()) : std::nullopt;
    }
};

Strategy #2: how we charge — and Decorator for the extras

class PricingStrategy {
public:
    virtual ~PricingStrategy() = default;
    virtual double price(std::chrono::minutes stay, VehicleType v) const = 0;
};

class HourlyPricing : public PricingStrategy {
    double perHour_;
public:
    explicit HourlyPricing(double r) : perHour_(r) {}
    double price(std::chrono::minutes stay, VehicleType) const override {
        long hours = (stay.count() + 59) / 60;      // round up to the next hour
        return perHour_ * static_cast<double>(hours);
    }
};

// Decorator: surcharges and discounts stack in any combination,
// without a subclass per combination.
class PricingDecorator : public PricingStrategy {
protected:

public:
    explicit PricingDecorator(std::unique_ptr<PricingStrategy> i) : inner_(std::move(i)) {}
};

class WeekendSurcharge : public PricingDecorator {
public:
    using PricingDecorator::PricingDecorator;
    double price(std::chrono::minutes s, VehicleType v) const override {
        return inner_->price(s, v) * 1.25;
    }
};

class ElectricDiscount : public PricingDecorator {
public:
    using PricingDecorator::PricingDecorator;
    double price(std::chrono::minutes s, VehicleType v) const override {

12. Problem 3: E-commerce cart and checkout

The one most likely to be asked at a retail company, and the one with the most classic traps.

Requirements

IN SCOPE                                OUT OF SCOPE
- browse products, add to cart          - the payment gateway itself
- promotions: % off, buy-2-get-1,       - shipping carrier integration
  free shipping over a threshold        - returns
- checkout creates an order
- stock must not oversell

SCALE: 1M products, read-heavy catalogue, spiky checkout traffic.
CONSISTENCY: stock and order creation must be exact; catalogue can be slightly stale.

The three traps, and how to walk into them deliberately

  1. Price must be snapshotted. order_line stores the price paid, not a join to the product's current price. Otherwise last year's invoices silently change when marketing runs a sale. State this as you write the table — it is the highest-value sentence in the problem.
  2. Product is not Inventory. Same abstract-vs-physical split as title/copy. A product is a catalogue entry; stock is per warehouse. And a variant (size, colour) is a third level that most candidates miss: you do not stock "T-shirt", you stock "T-shirt / Medium / Blue".
  3. Overselling is a race. Same defence as before: transaction, plus either a conditional update (UPDATE ... SET qty = qty - 1 WHERE qty >= 1, then check rows affected) or a CHECK (quantity >= 0) constraint so the database refuses to go negative.

Class design: promotions

Promotions are the varying part, so they get the pattern work. Two are in play, and knowing which to use where is the whole answer:

  • Strategy for "how is this one discount computed" — percentage, fixed amount, buy-2-get-1.
  • Decorator-style chaining for "apply several promotions in order", because promotions stack and the order matters.
#include <memory>
#include <vector>
#include <string>
#include <utility>

struct CartLine {
    long        variantId;
    int         quantity;
    double      unitPrice;
    std::string category;
};

struct PriceBreakdown {
    double subtotal = 0.0;
    double discount = 0.0;
    double shipping = 0.0;
    double total() const { return subtotal - discount + shipping; }
};

// ---- Strategy: one promotion rule --------------------------------------
class Promotion {
public:
    virtual ~Promotion() = default;
    virtual std::string name() const = 0;
    // returns the discount THIS promotion adds, given the cart and running totals
    virtual double discountFor(const std::vector<CartLine>& lines,
                               const PriceBreakdown& running) const = 0;
};

class PercentOffCategory : public Promotion {
    std::string category_;
    double      percent_;
public:
    PercentOffCategory(std::string c, double p)
        : category_(std::move(c)), percent_(p) {}
    std::string name() const override { return percent_ < 1 ? "PERCENT_OFF" : "PERCENT_OFF"; }
    double discountFor(const std::vector<CartLine>& lines,
                       const PriceBreakdown&) const override {
        double sum = 0.0;
        for (const auto& l : lines)
            if (l.category == category_) sum += l.unitPrice * l.quantity;
        return sum * percent_;
    }
};

class BuyTwoGetOneFree : public Promotion {
    std::string category_;
public:
    explicit BuyTwoGetOneFree(std::string c) : category_(std::move(c)) {}
    std::string name() const override { return "B2G1"; }
    double discountFor(const std::vector<CartLine>& lines,
                       const PriceBreakdown&) const override {
        double d = 0.0;
        for (const auto& l : lines)
            if (l.category == category_)
                d += (l.quantity / 3) * l.unitPrice;   // every 3rd item free
        return d;
    }
};

class FreeShippingOver : public Promotion {
    double threshold_;
public:
    explicit FreeShippingOver(double t) : threshold_(t) {}
    std::string name() const override { return "FREE_SHIPPING"; }
    double discountFor(const std::vector<CartLine>&,
                       const PriceBreakdown& running) const override {
        return running.subtotal >= threshold_ ? running.shipping : 0.0;
    }
};

// ---- The engine: applies promotions in a defined order ------------------
class PricingEngine {
    std::vector<std::unique_ptr<Promotion>> promotions_;   // order is significant
public:
    void add(std::unique_ptr<Promotion> p) { promotions_.push_back(std::move(p)); }

    PriceBreakdown price(const std::vector<CartLine>& lines, double baseShipping) const {
        PriceBreakdown b;
        for (const auto& l : lines) b.subtotal += l.unitPrice * l.quantity;
        b.shipping = baseShipping;

        for (const auto& p : promotions_)
            b.discount += p->discountFor(lines, b);

        if (b.discount > b.subtotal + b.shipping)     // never pay the customer
            b.discount = b.subtotal + b.shipping;
        return b;
    }
};

The trade-off to raise unprompted: "Promotions stacking means order matters — 10% off then £5 off is not the same as £5 off then 10% off. I've made the order explicit in the list rather than leaving it emergent. A real system usually also needs rules about which promotions can combine at all, and I'd model that as a priority plus an exclusivity group rather than hard-coding it."

13. Problem bank

Six more, in the form you would get them. Try each yourself for ten minutes before reading the solution — reading solutions creates recognition, not recall, and the interview tests recall.

Q1. Design an elevator control system

What it tests: the State pattern, and whether you conflate a single car with the bank.

Solution sketch. Two levels: Elevator (one car) and ElevatorController (dispatches across cars). Candidates who model only one car miss the interesting half.

State on the car: Idle, MovingUp, MovingDown, DoorsOpen, Maintenance. Each state answers "what happens on a request?" differently — a request while DoorsOpen queues, a request while Maintenance is rejected. That kills the big switch.

Strategy for dispatch: nearest-car, or the classic elevator algorithm (keep going in one direction serving requests until none remain that way). Swapping the strategy changes the whole behaviour of the bank, which is exactly why it should be an object.

class Elevator;                   // forward declarations
enum class Direction { Up, Down };

class ElevatorState {
public:
    virtual ~ElevatorState() = default;
    virtual void requestFloor(Elevator& e, int floor) = 0;
    virtual void tick(Elevator& e) = 0;
    virtual const char* name() const = 0;
};

class DispatchStrategy {
public:
    virtual ~DispatchStrategy() = default;
    virtual int chooseCar(const std::vector<Elevator>& cars, int floor, Direction d) const = 0;
};

Schema: mostly operational — elevator(id, bank_id, current_floor, state), trip_request(id, from_floor, to_floor, requested_at, served_by, served_at). Say the request log exists for analytics: average wait time is the business metric.

Q2. Design a movie ticket booking system

What it tests: concurrency on a shared scarce resource, and the abstract/physical split again.

The split: Movie (the film) vs Show (a screening at a time in a screen) vs Seat (physical, belongs to a screen) vs ShowSeat (this seat, at this show — the thing that is actually bookable). Missing ShowSeat is the classic error: seat 14A is not booked, it is booked for the 7pm showing.

show_seat (show_id FK, seat_id FK, status ENUM('AVAILABLE','HELD','BOOKED'),
           held_until TIMESTAMPTZ NULL, booking_id FK NULL,
           PRIMARY KEY (show_id, seat_id))

The concurrency answer — the point of the question. Seat selection needs a temporary hold, not an immediate booking, because the user needs a few minutes to pay. So: SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the chosen rows inside a transaction, set HELD with held_until = now() + 5 minutes, and have a sweeper (or a lazy check on read) release expired holds. Payment success converts hold to BOOKED. Mention that holds must expire, or a malicious user can freeze a whole cinema for free.

API: POST /shows/{id}/holds → 201 with an expiry; POST /bookings with the hold id and an idempotency key.

Q3. Design a notification service

What it tests: Observer plus Strategy, and whether you think about delivery guarantees.

Strategy per channel (EmailChannel, SmsChannel, PushChannel) behind one NotificationChannel interface. Observer so domain events fan out to whoever cares. Template Method for the send pipeline: render, validate, throttle, dispatch, record — with only render and dispatch varying.

struct Recipient;                 // forward declarations
struct RenderedMessage;

class NotificationChannel {
public:
    virtual ~NotificationChannel() = default;
    virtual bool send(const Recipient& to, const RenderedMessage& msg) = 0;
    virtual const char* channelName() const = 0;
};

Schema: notification(id, user_id, template_id, channel, status, attempts, created_at, sent_at, failed_reason), plus user_preference(user_id, channel, category, enabled) so users can mute categories. Add template(id, channel, locale, body) — and note the locale, because someone always forgets internationalisation.

The senior point: sending is unreliable, so it is a queue with retries and exponential backoff, an idempotency key per (event, user, channel) so a retried event does not double-send, and a dead-letter queue for permanent failures.

Q4. Design a file system

What it tests: Composite, plainly. Also whether you can handle recursion cleanly.

Composite exactly as in section 8: FileNode base, File leaf, Directory holding vector<unique_ptr<FileNode>>, with size() recursing. Add a Visitor-ish traversal if they push on "how would you search", or just an iterator.

Schema is the interesting twist — trees in SQL. Offer the options and pick:

  • Adjacency list — node(id, parent_id, name, type, size). Trivial to write, but "give me the full path" or "everything under this folder" needs a recursive query (WITH RECURSIVE).
  • Materialised path — store /a/b/c as a column. Subtree queries become a simple LIKE '/a/b/%'. Renaming a folder means rewriting descendants.
  • Closure table — a row per ancestor-descendant pair. Fast both directions, more storage and more write work.

Naming all three and choosing based on read/write mix is a strong answer. Add UNIQUE (parent_id, name) — you cannot have two files with the same name in one directory.

Q5. Design a vending machine

What it tests: State, and careful handling of money.

States: Idle, HasMoney, Dispensing, OutOfStock. Every input (insert coin, select item, cancel) means something different per state, which is precisely the State pattern's case. Draw the transition table — drawing it is worth more than describing it.

class Machine;                    // forward declaration

class VendingState {
public:
    virtual ~VendingState() = default;
    virtual void insertCoin(Machine&, int cents) = 0;
    virtual void select(Machine&, const std::string& slot) = 0;
    virtual void cancel(Machine&) = 0;
};

The details that separate answers: money in integer cents, never floating point; change calculation is a small coin-change problem and can fail (exact change only), which is a real state; and the dispense-then-decrement order matters if power can fail mid-transaction.

Q6. Design a ride-hailing service

What it tests: whether you keep this round's scope, since this is usually a System Design question.

Keep it in scope. Do not start with geo-sharding and Kafka. Model Rider, Driver, Vehicle, Trip, TripStatus, and the matching interface.

State on Trip: Requested → Matched → DriverArrived → InProgress → Completed, with Cancelled reachable from several — and note who is allowed to cancel changes by state, and whether a fee applies. Strategy for matching (nearest, highest-rated, best ETA) and for pricing (surge is a decorator over base fare).

14. Common mistakes and a self-check

The ten ways people lose this round

#MistakeWhat to do instead

Handling being challenged

They will push on something, and it is usually a test of how you think rather than a correction. The move is neither to cave instantly nor to dig in:

"That's a fair point — if <their scenario> happens, my design does <the bad thing>. I chose it because <original reason>, but if <their scenario> is common then <alternative> is better because <reason>. Which is closer to how your traffic actually looks?"

That acknowledges, explains, adapts, and hands the decision back with a real question. It works whether they were right or just probing.

Self-check against the stated rubric

The brief names three things. Before you say you are done, verify:

  • API design — did I give endpoints with methods, paths, a request body, a response body, at least one error case with a status code, and say something about pagination or idempotency?
  • Class design — does each class do one thing? Did I use composition where the behaviour varies? Did I justify each pattern by what would break without it? Did I write virtual ~Class()?
  • Data schema — did I name every primary key and foreign key, mark NOT NULL and UNIQUE, give at least one index with a reason, and handle the many-to-many with a join table?

The last five minutes — a script

Do not let the session peter out. Close deliberately:

"To summarise: <three sentences on the model>. If this grew 100×, the first thing to break would be <X>, and I'd fix it by <Y>. I deliberately left out <A and B>. The part I'm least sure about is <C> — I'd want to know more about <real-world constraint> before committing to it."

Naming the weakest part of your own design is counter-intuitive but reads as senior. It proves you evaluated it rather than just produced it.

The nine principles

1. Separate what varies from what stays the same
2. Program to an interface, not an implementation
3. Favor composition over inheritance
4. Strive for loosely coupled designs
5. Open for extension, closed for modification
6. Depend on abstractions, not concrete classes
7. Talk only to your immediate friends (Least Knowledge)
8. Don't call us, we'll call you (Hollywood)
9. One class, one reason to change

Schema reflexes

surrogate PK; natural key as UNIQUE
FK on the "many" side; join table for many-to-many (and it usually has its own columns)
DECIMAL for money, never FLOAT
TIMESTAMPTZ in UTC
snapshot price + name onto order lines
index every FK; composite index order = leftmost prefix rule
partial UNIQUE index to make double-booking impossible
CHECK constraints for invariants
created_at / updated_at on anything humans edit
